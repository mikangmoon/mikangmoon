QUEST 03 · UX RESEARCH CASE STUDY

Understanding Proof of Service

People didn’t struggle with a legal form. They struggled with the process behind it. I helped a civic design client see the difference.

THE 60-SECOND VERSION

THE PROBLEM

In small claims court, nobody has a lawyer. First-timers must complete a confusing form, “Proof of Service,” alone. Our client wanted to know where people get lost.

WHAT I DID

I designed the usability tasks and led 6 of 10 in-person sessions. Then I helped turn hours of observation into four findings and clear recommendations.

THE RESULT

The client began folding our recommendations into their working draft during our presentation, and a more refined form was on the way.

II owned

  • Designing the usability testing tasks and scenarios
  • Facilitating 6 of the 10 sessions, guiding think-aloud
  • Client communication, including walking through the service journey with them
  • Developing key insights and recommendations

WEWe did together

  • Recruiting 10 participants in person at a local library
  • Running the card-sorting timeline activity
  • Affinity mapping and synthesizing the findings
  • Presenting to the client
INTRO.EXE_□×

1 · THE CONTEXT

Small claims court settles money disputes of up to about $7,000, and lawyers can’t represent either side. People handle everything themselves, using forms designed to be accessible. Many still use dense language and unclear instructions.

That is a fairness problem. Complex legal terms can widen the power gap between citizens and the government. Courts across the country are working to fix it with better form design, plain language, and less legalese. Our client was one of them.

WHAT IS PROOF OF SERVICE?

After you file a claim, the other side must be formally handed the court papers. You can’t do it yourself. A third party, like a process server, delivers them. The Proof of Service form confirms it happened, and the case can’t reach a hearing without it.

OUR GOALEvaluate how users understand the service process, and find confusion across the whole service journey, not just the form.

TWO RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Where do users run into challenges, and what are they?

Is the process of filing a Proof of Service clear to the user?

We started with one broad question, “How do people understand Proof of Service?”, and split it in two with the client. That separated problems in the form itself from problems in the process around it.

CHALLENGES.EXE_□×

2 · THREE CHALLENGES

UNFAMILIAR DOMAIN

None of us knew legal documents, and small claims is a niche, complicated process. Grasping it was a personal challenge for me.

What I did: asked the client specific questions to walk us through the process, and studied how other countries design their proof-of-service documents.

NO TARGET USER GROUP

The client gave us no specific group to recruit from. We expected a defined audience.

What we did: reframed it. Small claims filers can be anyone, especially first-timers. So we recruited people randomly at a public library to get as varied a sample as possible.

A SERVICE, NOT A FORM

We planned to test the document itself. After talking with the client, we learned they wanted to evaluate the entire legal service journey.

What I did: moved from success-rate metrics to asking participants to explain their understanding of the process.

SO WHAT?Each challenge changed the study design. Randomized recruiting captured first-time filers, and the service-journey reframe led to the card-sorting timeline that produced our strongest evidence.
METHOD.EXE_□×

3 · HOW WE TESTED

We used usability testing to see how people move through the Proof of Service process, not just how they fill out the form. Each participant got a realistic scenario so they could imagine being part of a real legal process, then worked through the documents at their own pace. We watched what they did and heard what they thought, using only the context we gave them.

10

participants, in-person, 20–30 minutes each, recruited at a local library.

4

documents: the small claims form (filled and unfilled), a 6-page instructions packet, a blank sheet of paper, and a card-sorting packet.

6 of 10

sessions that I facilitated, encouraging participants to think aloud.

WHO TOOK PART

★ = legal background or prior small claims experience (3 of 10). Most participants had none, which matched our goal of understanding first-time behavior.

THE SCENARIO

“You moved out of your place on October 31st, 2025… On the move-out date you returned the key to your landlord… They never returned your $1,500 security deposit.” Participants imagined filing a small claim for it.

THE TIMELINE ACTIVITY

At the end of each session, participants arranged cards to map the whole service journey on a timeline. It captured their mental model directly, so we could see how they believed the steps fit together.

SO WHAT?Paper forms have no clicks or time-on-task to measure. Asking people to talk aloud and build a timeline gave us what metrics couldn’t: how they understood the system.
PROCESS.EXE_□×

4 · THE PROCESS

ALIGN WITH THE CLIENT

We met to agree on expectations and goals. I shared our team’s confusion about the document, and we walked through the service journey together.

WRITE THE TASKS

I designed scenarios and tasks that made participants imagine the situation for real, using both unfilled and filled-in documents.

RUN THE SESSIONS

I let participants use the documents however they wanted. Their behavior showed what mattered to them, like highlighting words that sounded important.

BUILD THE AFFINITY WALL

We gathered to analyze every note together. I added specific participant behaviors next to what they said about the document.

THE AFFINITY WALL

We broke each session into individual quotes on blue notes, grouped them into categories, and labeled themes on pink and yellow notes. We sorted by what participants did and said, not by sections of the form.

SO WHAT?Sorting by behavior instead of by form section is why our findings are about the process, not just the paper. Four themes emerged, and each one became a finding.
FINDINGS.EXE_□×

5 · WHAT WE FOUND

THE BIG PICTUREThe first reaction to the form was “clear.” But understanding of the process behind it left people very confused.

FINDING 1

USERS LACK A CLEAR MENTAL MODEL OF THE PROCESS

Participants, especially those with no legal experience, were unsure what Proof of Service is, why they’d fill it out, and how it fits the wider legal process. The confusion was foundational, not limited to one section. Many assumed the plaintiff, defendant, or court clerk completed every part.

“I’d be lost if I had to fill the proof of service out on my own.”

“I think this is an insurance claim?”

“Could be helpful if it was more clearly marked where who was filling in.”

The timeline evidence: every participant built a different timeline, and no two matched. That showed there is no shared, accurate picture of the process.

FINDING 2

FORM DESIGN INTRODUCES AMBIGUITY AND ERRORS

The checkboxes at the top of the form were the sticking point. The horizontal layout left people unsure whether to pick one option or several. Similar choices like “I served” and “personally” blurred together. People hesitated and second-guessed instead of answering with confidence.

“I served and personally, it’s the same dang thing!”

“I always believe when I’m filling out a form that I should check one box.”

FINDING 3

TERMINOLOGY CREATES COMPREHENSION BARRIERS

Terms like process server, notary, and deputy clerk never appeared in the earlier small claims form. Participants stopped completing the task to decode vocabulary, which made the experience more overwhelming.

“What is a Deputy Clerk and notary signature means and what do they do?”

“I don’t get the ‘Find a process server’ phrase.”

FINDING 4

UNCLEAR FEES CREATE ANXIETY

Many participants were surprised there was a service fee at all. They were also unsure who pays it and who receives the money, which made first-timers hesitant.

“I guess whoever loses the case would pay for the proof of service fee.”

“I didn’t know they serve a fee out there.”

ALSO NOTICEDOne out-of-scope observation: nothing says the “Acknowledgement of Service” section is optional, so participants wondered why it was blank and whether the defendant had received the paper.
RECOMMENDATIONS.EXE_□×

6 · WHAT WE RECOMMENDED

Each recommendation answers one finding, so the client could see exactly why we suggested it.

FINDING 1 → RECOMMENDATION 1

Explain the form up top, and show the whole journey.

  • Add a “What is Proof of Service?” explanation at the top: “Proof of Service means officially delivering your court papers to the defendant. This form proves to the court that the defendant received the documents.” Also say who’s involved, when it must be filed, and why it matters.
  • Include a step-by-step visual timeline, from Step 1 (file a claim) to Step 7 (the case proceeds after service is complete).
  • Clarify roles: plaintiff, process server, and court. For example, “Process Server = physically delivers the documents to the defendant.”
  • Offer a one-page guide to the main steps, and an annotated sample showing how a process server completes the form.

Why: it sets the purpose before people meet legal language, builds the right mental model from the start, and lowers anxiety for first-time filers. It also makes the third-party process server, whom many participants overlooked, visible.

FINDING 2 → RECOMMENDATION 2

Remove the guesswork from the checkboxes.

  • State the selection rule clearly, such as “select one.”
  • Restructure the checkbox flow to match how people expect forms to work, and order options so each builds logically on the last.

Why: the form asked people to interpret too much, which raised cognitive load and the chance of mistakes.

FINDING 3 → RECOMMENDATION 3

Define new terms where people meet them.

  • Define terms and roles early in the instructions, limiting how much new information arrives at once.
  • Embed short definitions right in the form, like “You will need a process server, who is responsible for delivering the documents…”

Why: readers get context exactly when they need it, without leaving the form to look it up.

FINDING 4 → RECOMMENDATION 4

Make the fee transparent.

  • Explain what the fee is for, how it’s calculated, why it’s charged, who pays it, and who receives the payment.

Why: people shouldn’t have to assume or search for answers, which was the source of the anxiety.

IMPACT.EXE_□×

7 · IMPACT

PROJECT IMPACT (WHAT HAPPENED)

  • During our presentation, the client began incorporating our recommendations into their working draft, in real time.
  • Our recommendations informed the next iteration of the form.
  • The client shared that a more refined draft was expected in June.

USER IMPACT (WHAT WE EXPECT)

  • Better form clarity and completion accuracy
  • Lower cognitive load for first-time users
  • More transparency and confidence going through a legal process
SO WHAT?The work did more than flag problems. It influenced ongoing improvements to a real legal document that people use without a lawyer.
REFLECTION.EXE_□×

8 · WHAT I TOOK AWAY

GETTING COMFORTABLE WITH AMBIGUITY

At the start, it wasn’t clear whether the problem was the form or the broader process. I learned to break the problem down, ask clarifying questions, and keep checking in. Research isn’t only about users. It’s also about making sure stakeholders agree on the problem.

FROM INTERFACE TO SERVICE THINKING

Users weren’t just struggling with the form. They were struggling to understand the process around it. So I stopped measuring task completion and looked at how people mentally map the steps. An interface affects the entire service journey.

TESTING A NON-DIGITAL EXPERIENCE

With a paper form, I couldn’t lean on typical UX data. I relied on watching where participants hesitated, how they read instructions, and what they said aloud. It sharpened my eye for subtle behavior beyond what metrics show.

THE BIG LESSONZoom out. Good user research isn’t just about improving interfaces. It’s about understanding the whole system people are navigating.
