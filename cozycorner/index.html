THE 60-SECOND VERSION

THE PROBLEM

Despite 1 in 4 adults facing a mental health challenge each year, there remains a gap between understanding the importance of mental health and being able to access support right when it’s needed. In our survey, 60% of respondents reported wishing they had tools available exactly during these “critical moments” that shape their emotional well-being.

THE SOLUTION

CozyCorner turns support into a cozy video game. Players chat anonymously with real therapists and peers, play calming mini-games, and find resources in one app.

WHAT I DELIVERED

Designed and validated a complete product concept from scratch in eight weeks, delivering data-driven surveys, wireframes, a high-fidelity design system, and two interactive prototypes. Currently expanding the concept as an ongoing passion project to further refine the user experience.

RESEARCH.EXE_□×

1 · WHAT WE LEARNED

Research question: How might a gamified digital therapy support young adults’ mental health when traditional help is inaccessible?

We focused on critical moments, like panic before a final exam or dread after a long workday. People often dismiss these as “just a feeling,” so they go unsupported.

For our research, we used a combination of secondary and primary methods to better understand the mental health support landscape and user needs. Because we had a very limited time, our primary research method was survey for gathering direct user insights.

  • Literature review: sharpened the question and the audience.
  • Competitive analysis: showed most apps push long-term programs, not help in the moment.
  • Survey: 30 young adults on Google Forms.
SO WHAT?The competitor gap, plus survey answers, pointed us to one bet: immediate, anonymous, human support.

WHO WE ASKED

I split responses into these three groups to see whether needs shifted with life stage. Our participants came from our own networks, so this is directional, not representative (see limits below).

SURVEY INSIGHTS

To guide our research and develop our user survey, we broke our main research question into three key survey questions we wanted answers for:

QUESTION 1

What barriers do young adults face when trying to access immediate mental health support?

QUESTION 2

How can digital tools help support emotional well-being during critical moments?

QUESTION 3

How might we design a gamified therapy platform that makes mental health support more accessible and engaging?

WHAT THEY TOLD US → WHAT I DESIGNED

NEED FOR HUMAN CONNECTION

“Availability of human resources. AI tools are always available, but have a lot of pitfalls.”

→ Connect with real players and therapists, not just bots.

HARD TO BE VULNERABLE

“I find it’s difficult to open up about struggles, even to the people that I’m close to and know well.”

→ An anonymous, safe space where avatars replace names and photos.

LONG WAIT TIMES

“I wish I didn’t have to make an appointment or go through paperwork to see a licensed professional and be anonymous.”

→ Help anytime, anywhere: connect in seconds, no scheduling or forms.

ACCESS BARRIERS

“Making it more accessible & bring awareness so people know about the resources.”

→ Easy access to resources: curated tools and exercises in one app.

SO WHAT?These four pairs became CozyCorner’s four core features. Each feature maps to a finding, not a hunch.
DESIGN_SYSTEM.EXE_□×

2 · MAKING IT FEEL SAFE

We wanted the app to feel comforting, not clinical.

  • Logo: a couch (a place to unwind) plus a star (gentle guidance).
  • Type: rounded Fredoka stays friendly and readable for someone overwhelmed.
  • Color: soft pastels lower visual stress.
  • Icons and keyboard: consistent and game-like, so navigation is effortless.
SO WHAT?Survey respondents feared judgment and felt overwhelmed. Every visual choice lowers that barrier before a single word is read.

THE HOME ECOSYSTEM

Home is both the dashboard and the avatar’s actual room. Clicking the door opens a shared social street. The computer opens mini-games. A bottom bar sits in thumb reach for the therapy chat and other tools.

SO WHAT?Mapping the flow showed every feature hangs off one hub. Users choose when to stay private (home) or step out (street).
WIREFRAMES.EXE_□×

3 · FROM STRUCTURE TO FEELING

I built the mid-fi wireframes with the other UX designer, focusing on layout and flow before visual style.

Home → game picker → Wordle → completion screen. I split games into multiplayer and single-player so users pick the mode that fits their mood.

The social street is still in progress. Moves are simple (walk, wave, tap to request a chat) so connecting stays low-pressure.

SO WHAT?Testing the flow on paper let us fix navigation before spending time on visuals.

HI-FI: THE COZY LOOK

I applied the palette while checking text stayed readable. One small change mattered most: I relabeled game modes from “multiplayer” and “single player” to “play together” and “need some alone time”, words that match how someone actually feels in the moment.

PROTOTYPES.EXE_□×

4 · TRY THE PROTOTYPES

Press play to watch each one. Animations start paused.

WELLNESS WORDLE

I chose Wordle because young adults already know it, and it takes little effort, so even non-gamers can join. Each round teaches mental health vocabulary and positive affirmations.

ANONYMOUS THERAPY CHAT

Users reach real professionals in seconds. I added a 1–10 emotion scale so people can show how they feel without finding the words. The look is game-like on purpose, to avoid a clinical tone.

SO WHAT?These two prototypes cover all four goals: immediate support, emotional safety, easy resources, and real human connection.
WHATS_NEXT.EXE_□×

5 · STILL BUILDING

Since the competition, I’ve kept going on my own. I’m building user profiles with a wellness tracker and reflection games, so players can see their well-being over time. I’ve used Lovable and Figma Make to explore layouts quickly, then refined them by hand.

WHAT I’D DO NEXT, AND WHY

ACCESSIBILITY SETTINGS

Adjustable color palettes and visual settings. Accessibility was a founding goal, so it should be a feature.

ONBOARDING

Tutorials, goals, and character customization to give users autonomy from the first minute.

MORE GAMES, MORE COMMUNITY

Multiplayer features for anonymous peer support during hard moments.

DEEPER RESEARCH

User interviews to validate the survey, plus usability tests on mid-fi flows to catch friction earlier.

HONEST LIMITSWe used convenience sampling (people we knew), ran one primary study, and designed a small feature set. The findings are directional. The next steps above close exactly those gaps.
TAKEAWAYS.EXE_□×

WHAT THIS TAUGHT ME

MOVE FAST, ADAPT FASTER

This project taught me how different stages of a product can influence the app and the importance of keeping up with a fast-paced environment. I learned to maximize my time, work efficiently, and adapt quickly when priorities or constraints changed, which is essential in real-world product and UX work.

I’M NOT THE USER

I realized that I am not the user of the product, and assuming what users want can lead to biased designs. Starting with research was critical to understand users’ needs and build empathy. This approach helped me eliminate initial assumptions, ensuring that design decisions were informed by real user perspectives rather than my own preferences.

DIVERSE TEAMS WIN

Collaboration proved to be essential, especially when navigating unfamiliar territory. Stepping into a product manager role for the first time pushed me to consider not only design decisions but also business implications. Working closely with a business analyst helped me understand market sizing while collaborating with another UX designer allowed for constant feedback. This experience sharpened my design thinking skills and highlighted the value of diverse perspectives in creating well-rounded products.
